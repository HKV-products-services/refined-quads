Refined quad meshes

A refined quad mesh is a 2D mesh of axis-aligned squares on a fixed lattice, locally refined by repeated halving, with triangles only where cell sizes change.

Base cell, refinement level and lattice

The base cell is the coarsest square; its size is any positive length, for example 32 m or 25 m. Each refinement level halves it once: level 0 is the base cell, level L has side cell size / 2**L. A 25 m base cell gives 12.5, 6.25 and 3.125 m cells at levels 1 to 3.

The lattice is the fixed grid of points on which every square has its corners: an origin and the base cell. By default the origin is the lower-left corner of the domain rounded down to a multiple of the base cell, so meshes with the same base cell line up. Internally every corner is an integer index on the finest level of the lattice, so coordinates are computed once, as origin + index * unit, and comparisons are exact.

A lattice square is a square of the quadtree: a base cell or one of the squares its halving gives. It becomes one square cell, or is filled by transition cells.

Transition rows and transition cells

Around a refinement area the mesh does not jump from fine to coarse at once. Transition rows set, per step from coarse to fine, how many rows of finer cells surround the finer area before the mesh coarsens. With 0 only the cells touching the area get its level and the 2:1 balance makes the transition; that gives the fewest cells. (The old generator, meshgen, counted 0 as 1: its buffer_rows 0 is transition_rows=1 here.)

Neighbouring lattice squares differ at most one level (2:1 balance). Where a finer square meets a coarser one, the coarser square gets a node in the middle of that side and is split into transition cells: one split side gives a fan of three triangles, two adjacent split sides four triangles, and two opposite or three or four split sides four squares around the centre. Together they fill the lattice square exactly, so there are no hanging nodes (nodes in the middle of another cell’s edge).

Domain and water

The domain encloses the mesh. A lattice square that overlaps it is kept whole, so the outer edge follows the lattice rather than the polygon exactly. Water areas and holes in the domain are clipped out of the finished mesh cell by cell (see Water areas); after that some lattice squares are filled only partly, which is how the remaining cells follow the water’s edge.
